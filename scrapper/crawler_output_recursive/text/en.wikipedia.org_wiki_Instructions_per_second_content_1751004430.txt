Measure of a computer's processing speed
[![](//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/59/Computing_efficiency%2C_OWID.svg/500px-Computing_efficiency%2C_OWID.svg.png)](/wiki/File:Computing_efficiency,_OWID.svg)Computer processing efficiency, measured as the power needed per million instructions per second (watts per MIPS) **Instructions per second **( **IPS **) is a measure of a [computer ](/wiki/Computer)'s [processor ](/wiki/Central_processing_unit)speed. For [complex instruction set computers ](/wiki/Complex_instruction_set_computer)(CISCs), different [instructions ](/wiki/Machine_code)take different amounts of time, so the value measured depends on the instruction mix; even for comparing processors in the same family the IPS measurement can be problematic. Many reported IPS values have represented "peak" execution rates on artificial instruction sequences with few [branches ](/wiki/Subroutine)and no [cache contention ](/wiki/Resource_contention), whereas realistic workloads typically lead to significantly lower IPS values. [Memory hierarchy ](/wiki/Memory_hierarchy)also greatly affects processor performance, an issue barely considered in IPS calculations. Because of these problems, synthetic [benchmarks ](/wiki/Benchmark_(computing))such as [Dhrystone ](/wiki/Dhrystone)are now generally used to estimate [computer performance ](/wiki/Computer_performance)in commonly used applications, and raw IPS has fallen into disuse.
The term is commonly used in association with a [metric prefix ](/wiki/Metric_prefix)(k, M, G, T, P, or E) to form **kilo instructions per second **( **kIPS **), **mega instructions per second **( **MIPS **), **giga instructions per second **( **GIPS **) and so on. Formerly **TIPS **was used occasionally for "thousand IPS".
## Computing
[ [edit ](/w/index.php?title=Instructions_per_second&action=edit&section=1)]
IPS can be calculated using this equation: ^[[ 1 ] ](#cite_note-en.community.dell.com-1) IPS = sockets × cores socket × clock × Is cycle {\displaystyle {\text{IPS}}={\text{sockets}}\times {\frac {\text{cores}}{\text{socket}}}\times {\text{clock}}\times {\frac {\text{Is}}{\text{cycle}}}} ![{\displaystyle {\text{IPS}}={\text{sockets}}\times {\frac {\text{cores}}{\text{socket}}}\times {\text{clock}}\times {\frac {\text{Is}}{\text{cycle}}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/720710a1e11cfb9dfd87f4e11df7906f6c27ec3c)
However, the instructions/cycle measurement depends on the instruction sequence, the data and external factors.
## Scaling units
[ [edit ](/w/index.php?title=Instructions_per_second&action=edit&section=2)]
For the most early [8-bit ](/wiki/8-bit_computing)and [16-bit ](/wiki/16-bit_computing)[microprocessors ](/wiki/Microprocessor), performance was measured in [thousand ](/wiki/1000_(number))instructions per second, or kilo instructions per second (kIPS).
The term "mega instructions per second" became useful in the late 1970s. The [IBM ](/wiki/IBM)[System/370 ](/wiki/System/370)model 158–3 and the [VAX-11/780 ](/wiki/VAX-11#VAX-11/780)were considered roughly equivalent at 1 MIPS.
## Types of instruction
[ [edit ](/w/index.php?title=Instructions_per_second&action=edit&section=3)]
The speed of a given CPU depends on many factors, such as the type of instructions being executed, the execution order and the presence of branch instructions (problematic in CPU pipelines). CPU instruction rates are different from clock frequencies, usually reported in [Hz ](/wiki/Hertz), as each instruction may require several clock cycles to complete or the processor may be capable of executing multiple independent instructions simultaneously. MIPS can be useful when comparing performance between processors made with similar architecture (e.g. [PIC microcontrollers ](/wiki/PIC_microcontrollers)), but they are difficult to compare between differing [CPU architectures ](/wiki/CPU_architecture), especially between [RISC ](/wiki/RISC)and [CISC ](/wiki/Complex_instruction_set_computer)architectures. ^[[ 2 ] ](#cite_note-2) This led to the term "Meaningless Indicator of Processor Speed," ^[[ 3 ] ](#cite_note-3) or less commonly, "Meaningless Indices of Performance," ^[[ 4 ] ](#cite_note-4) being popular amongst technical people by the mid-1980s.
### The Gibson Mix (1959)
[ [edit ](/w/index.php?title=Instructions_per_second&action=edit&section=4)]
Before standard benchmarks were available, average speed rating of computers was based on calculations for a mix of instructions with the results given in [kilo ](/wiki/Kilo-)instructions per second (kIPS). Among the first attempts at defining a specific collections of instructions to time was the **Gibson Mix **, produced by Jack Clark Gibson of IBM for scientific applications in 1959. ^[[ 5 ] ](#cite_note-5)
Gibson divided computer instructions into 12 classes, based on the [IBM 704 ](/wiki/IBM_704)architecture, adding a 13th class to account for indexing time. Weights were primarily based on analysis of seven scientific programs run on the 704, with a small contribution from some [IBM 650 ](/wiki/IBM_650)programs. The overall score was then the weighted sum of the average execution speed for instructions in each class. ^[[ 6 ] ](#cite_note-6)

The Gibson Mix is a product of its era, when computer speeds were still measured in kIPS.
Other ratings, such as the ADP mix which does not include floating point operations,  were produced for commercial applications.
These early ratings were nowhere as systemic as the later approaches, although the idea of creating a benchmark to resemble real-world applications (later called a "synthetic benchmark") would persist.
### Two flavors of VAX MIPS
[ [edit ](/w/index.php?title=Instructions_per_second&action=edit&section=5)]
The VAX-11/780 was released in 1977. It was marketed as being able to execute the equivalant of 1 million System/370 instructions per second, the first minicomputer to achieve such a speed. VAX-11/780 would quickly become a unit of reference for MIPS measurements, in two separate benchmarks:
  - The [Whetstone benchmark ](/wiki/Whetstone_benchmark)of 1972 was modified in 1980 to combine three of its integer-operation speed measures into a "VAX MIPS". It originally included a mix of 42 statements written in ALGOL 60 (124 instructions on the KDF9 compiler), though by 1980 it had been rewritten in Fortran.
  - The integer-and-string-heavy [Dhrystone ](/wiki/Dhrystone)benchmark of 1984 inherited the idea of using VAX as a MIPS reference. Its results were reported in "DMIPS", for Dhrystone MIPS. Each Dhrystone MIPS was defined as the ability to run the Dhrystone main loop 1757 times per second, the score VAX-11/780 received on this benchmark.
### Other instruction mixes
[ [edit ](/w/index.php?title=Instructions_per_second&action=edit&section=6)] *zMIPS *refers to the MIPS measure used internally by [IBM ](/wiki/IBM)to rate its [mainframe ](/wiki/Mainframe_computer)servers ( [zSeries ](/wiki/ZSeries), [IBM System z9 ](/wiki/IBM_System_z9), and [IBM System z10 ](/wiki/IBM_System_z10)). *Weighted million operations per second (WMOPS) *is a similar measurement, used for audio codecs.
## Other factors that affect instructions per second
[ [edit ](/w/index.php?title=Instructions_per_second&action=edit&section=7)]
Effective MIPS speeds are highly dependent on the programming language used: some compilers generate highly-efficient code, others do not. The Whetstone Report has a table showing MWIPS speeds of PCs via early interpreters and compilers up to modern languages. The first PC compiler was for [BASIC ](/wiki/BASIC)(1982) when a 4.8 MHz 8088/87 CPU obtained 0.01 MWIPS. Results on a 2.4 GHz Intel Core 2 Duo (1 CPU 2007) vary from 9.7 MWIPS using BASIC Interpreter, 59 MWIPS via BASIC Compiler, 347 MWIPS using 1987 Fortran, 1,534 MWIPS through HTML/Java to 2,403 MWIPS using a modern [C ](/wiki/C_(programming_language))/ [C++ ](/wiki/C%2B%2B)compiler.
