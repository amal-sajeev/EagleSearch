"Operations per second" redirects here and is not to be confused with [Instructions per second ](/wiki/Instructions_per_second). **Floating point operations per second **( **FLOPS **, **flops **or **flop/s **) is a measure of [computer performance ](/wiki/Computer_performance)in [computing ](/wiki/Computing), useful in fields of scientific computations that require [floating-point ](/wiki/Floating-point)calculations. ^[[ 1 ] ](#cite_note-1)
For such cases, it is a more accurate measure than [instructions per second ](/wiki/Instructions_per_second). ^[ *[citation needed ](/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed)*]
## Floating-point arithmetic
[ [edit ](/w/index.php?title=Floating_point_operations_per_second&action=edit&section=1)]

[Floating-point arithmetic ](/wiki/Floating-point_arithmetic)is needed for very large or very small [real numbers ](/wiki/Real_number), or computations that require a large dynamic range. Floating-point representation is similar to scientific notation, except computers use [base two ](/wiki/Binary_number)(with rare exceptions), rather than [base ten ](/wiki/Decimal). The encoding scheme stores the sign, the [exponent ](/wiki/Exponent)(in base two for Cray and [VAX ](/wiki/VAX), base two or ten for [IEEE floating point ](/wiki/IEEE_floating_point)formats, and base 16 for [IBM Floating Point Architecture ](/wiki/IBM_hexadecimal_floating-point)) and the [significand ](/wiki/Significand)(number after the [radix point ](/wiki/Radix_point)). While several similar formats are in use, the most common is [ANSI/IEEE Std. 754-1985 ](/wiki/IEEE_754-1985). This standard defines the format for 32-bit numbers called *single precision *, as well as 64-bit numbers called *double precision *and longer numbers called *extended precision *(used for intermediate results). Floating-point representations can support a much wider range of values than fixed-point, with the ability to represent very small numbers and very large numbers. ^[[ 2 ] ](#cite_note-2)
### Dynamic range and precision
[ [edit ](/w/index.php?title=Floating_point_operations_per_second&action=edit&section=2)]
The exponentiation inherent in floating-point computation assures a much larger dynamic range – the largest and smallest numbers that can be represented – which is especially important when processing data sets where some of the data may have extremely large range of numerical values or where the range may be unpredictable. As such, floating-point processors are ideally suited for computationally intensive applications. ^[[ 3 ] ](#cite_note-3)
### Computational performance
[ [edit ](/w/index.php?title=Floating_point_operations_per_second&action=edit&section=3)]
FLOPS and [MIPS ](/wiki/Million_instructions_per_second#Million_instructions_per_second)are units of measure for the numerical computing performance of a computer. Floating-point operations are typically used in fields such as scientific computational research, as well as in [machine learning ](/wiki/Machine_learning). However, before the late 1980s floating-point hardware (it's possible to implement FP arithmetic in software over any integer hardware) was typically an optional feature, and computers that had it were said to be "scientific computers", or to have " [scientific computation ](/wiki/Scientific_computation)" capability. Thus the unit MIPS was useful to measure integer performance of any computer, including those without such a capability, and to account for architecture differences, similar MOPS (million operations per second) was used as early as 1970 ^[[ 4 ] ](#cite_note-4) as well. Note that besides integer (or fixed-point) arithmetics, examples of integer operation include data movement (A to B) or value testing (If A = B, then C). That's why MIPS as a performance benchmark is adequate when a computer is used in database queries, word processing, spreadsheets, or to run multiple virtual operating systems. ^[[ 5 ] ](#cite_note-5) ^[[ 6 ] ](#cite_note-6) In 1974 [David Kuck ](/wiki/David_Kuck)coined the terms flops and megaflops for the description of supercomputer performance of the day by the number of floating-point calculations they performed per second. ^[[ 7 ] ](#cite_note-7) This was much better than using the prevalent MIPS to compare computers as this statistic usually had little bearing on the arithmetic capability of the machine on scientific tasks. [![](//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/83/Supercomputer_Power_%28FLOPS%29%2C_OWID.svg/500px-Supercomputer_Power_%28FLOPS%29%2C_OWID.svg.png)](/wiki/File:Supercomputer_Power_(FLOPS),_OWID.svg)FLOPS by the largest [supercomputer ](/wiki/Supercomputer)over time
FLOPS on an HPC-system can be calculated using this equation: ^[[ 8 ] ](#cite_note-en.community.dell.com-8) FLOPS = racks × nodes rack × sockets node × cores socket × cycles second × FLOPs cycle . {\displaystyle {\text{FLOPS}}={\text{racks}}\times {\frac {\text{nodes}}{\text{rack}}}\times {\frac {\text{sockets}}{\text{node}}}\times {\frac {\text{cores}}{\text{socket}}}\times {\frac {\text{cycles}}{\text{second}}}\times {\frac {\text{FLOPs}}{\text{cycle}}}.} ![{\displaystyle {\text{FLOPS}}={\text{racks}}\times {\frac {\text{nodes}}{\text{rack}}}\times {\frac {\text{sockets}}{\text{node}}}\times {\frac {\text{cores}}{\text{socket}}}\times {\frac {\text{cycles}}{\text{second}}}\times {\frac {\text{FLOPs}}{\text{cycle}}}.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/b4c3cab47d00c423aa59c2660fe866d7f1c998c9)
This can be simplified to the most common case: a computer that has exactly 1 CPU: FLOPS = cores × cycles second × FLOPs cycle . {\displaystyle {\text{FLOPS}}={\text{cores}}\times {\frac {\text{cycles}}{\text{second}}}\times {\frac {\text{FLOPs}}{\text{cycle}}}.} ![{\displaystyle {\text{FLOPS}}={\text{cores}}\times {\frac {\text{cycles}}{\text{second}}}\times {\frac {\text{FLOPs}}{\text{cycle}}}.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/1bbd29a73aeda25c5d0c4b7fb174ddb971bca341)
FLOPS can be recorded in different measures of precision, for example, the [TOP500 ](/wiki/TOP500)supercomputer list ranks computers by 64-bit ( [double-precision floating-point format ](/wiki/Double-precision_floating-point_format)) operations per second, abbreviated to *FP64 *. ^[[ 9 ] ](#cite_note-top500faq-9) Similar measures are available for [32-bit ](/wiki/Single-precision_floating-point_format)( *FP32 *) and [16-bit ](/wiki/Half-precision_floating-point_format)( *FP16 *) operations.

## Performance records
[ [edit ](/w/index.php?title=Floating_point_operations_per_second&action=edit&section=5)]
### Single computer records
[ [edit ](/w/index.php?title=Floating_point_operations_per_second&action=edit&section=6)]
In June 1997, [Intel ](/wiki/Intel)'s [ASCI Red ](/wiki/ASCI_Red)was the world's first computer to achieve one teraFLOPS and beyond. Sandia director Bill Camp said that ASCI Red had the best reliability of any supercomputer ever built, and "was supercomputing's high-water mark in longevity, price, and performance". ^[[ 40 ] ](#cite_note-jacobsequity.com-40)
[NEC ](/wiki/NEC)'s [SX-9 ](/wiki/NEC_SX-9)supercomputer was the world's first [vector processor ](/wiki/Vector_processor)to exceed 100 gigaFLOPS per single core.
In June 2006, a new computer was announced by Japanese research institute [RIKEN ](/wiki/RIKEN), the [MDGRAPE-3 ](/wiki/MDGRAPE-3). The computer's performance tops out at one petaFLOPS, almost two times faster than the Blue Gene/L, but MDGRAPE-3 is not a general purpose computer, which is why it does not appear in the [Top500.org ](/wiki/TOP500)list. It has special-purpose [pipelines ](/wiki/Pipeline_(computing))for simulating molecular dynamics.
By 2007, [Intel Corporation ](/wiki/Intel)unveiled the experimental [multi-core ](/wiki/Multi-core)[POLARIS ](/wiki/Teraflops_Research_Chip)chip, which achieves 1 teraFLOPS at 3.13 GHz. The 80-core chip can raise this result to 2 teraFLOPS at 6.26 GHz, although the thermal dissipation at this frequency exceeds 190 watts. ^[[ 41 ] ](#cite_note-41)
In June 2007, Top500.org reported the fastest computer in the world to be the [IBM Blue Gene/L ](/wiki/Blue_Gene)supercomputer, measuring a peak of 596 teraFLOPS. ^[[ 42 ] ](#cite_note-42) The [Cray XT4 ](/wiki/Cray_XT4)hit second place with 101.7 teraFLOPS.
On June 26, 2007, [IBM ](/wiki/IBM)announced the second generation of its top supercomputer, dubbed Blue Gene/P and designed to continuously operate at speeds exceeding one petaFLOPS, faster than the Blue Gene/L. When configured to do so, it can reach speeds in excess of three petaFLOPS. ^[[ 43 ] ](#cite_note-43)
On October 25, 2007, [NEC ](/wiki/NEC)Corporation of Japan issued a press release announcing its SX series model [SX-9 ](/wiki/SX-9), ^[[ 44 ] ](#cite_note-44) claiming it to be the world's fastest vector supercomputer. The [SX-9 ](/wiki/SX-9)features the first CPU capable of a peak vector performance of 102.4 gigaFLOPS per single core.
On February 4, 2008, the [NSF ](/wiki/National_Science_Foundation)and the [University of Texas at Austin ](/wiki/University_of_Texas_at_Austin)opened full scale research runs on an [AMD ](/wiki/AMD), [Sun ](/wiki/Sun_Microsystems)supercomputer named Ranger, ^[[ 45 ] ](#cite_note-45) the most powerful supercomputing system in the world for open science research, which operates at sustained speed of 0.5 petaFLOPS.
On May 25, 2008, an American supercomputer built by [IBM ](/wiki/IBM), named ' [Roadrunner ](/wiki/IBM_Roadrunner)', reached the computing milestone of one petaFLOPS. It headed the June 2008 and November 2008 [TOP500 ](/wiki/TOP500)list of the most powerful supercomputers (excluding [grid computers ](/wiki/Grid_computing)). ^[[ 46 ] ](#cite_note-46) ^[[ 47 ] ](#cite_note-47) The computer is located at Los Alamos National Laboratory in New Mexico. The computer's name refers to the New Mexico [state bird ](/wiki/List_of_U.S._state_birds), the [greater roadrunner ](/wiki/Greater_roadrunner)( *Geococcyx californianus *). ^[[ 48 ] ](#cite_note-48)
In June 2008, AMD released ATI Radeon HD 4800 series, which are reported to be the first GPUs to achieve one teraFLOPS. On August 12, 2008, AMD released the ATI Radeon HD 4870X2 graphics card with two [Radeon R770 ](/wiki/Radeon_HD_4000_series)GPUs totaling 2.4 teraFLOPS.
In November 2008, an upgrade to the Cray [Jaguar supercomputer ](/wiki/Jaguar_(supercomputer))at the Department of Energy's (DOE's) Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) raised the system's computing power to a peak 1.64 petaFLOPS, making Jaguar the world's first petaFLOPS system dedicated to [open research ](/wiki/Open_research). In early 2009 the supercomputer was named after a mythical creature, [Kraken ](/wiki/Kraken). Kraken was declared the world's fastest university-managed supercomputer and sixth fastest overall in the 2009 TOP500 list. In 2010 Kraken was upgraded and can operate faster and is more powerful.
In 2009, the [Cray ](/wiki/Cray)Jaguar performed at 1.75 petaFLOPS, beating the IBM Roadrunner for the number one spot on the [TOP500 ](/wiki/TOP500)list. ^[[ 49 ] ](#cite_note-49)
In October 2010, China unveiled the [Tianhe-1 ](/wiki/Tianhe-1), a supercomputer that operates at a peak computing rate of 2.5 petaFLOPS. ^[[ 50 ] ](#cite_note-50) ^[[ 51 ] ](#cite_note-51)
As of 2010 ^[[update] ](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Floating_point_operations_per_second&action=edit) the fastest PC [processor ](/wiki/Microprocessor)reached 109 gigaFLOPS (Intel Core i7 [980 XE ](/wiki/Gulftown_(microprocessor))) ^[[ 52 ] ](#cite_note-52) in double precision calculations. [GPUs ](/wiki/Graphics_processing_unit)are considerably more powerful. For example, [Nvidia Tesla ](/wiki/Nvidia_Tesla)C2050 GPU computing processors perform around 515 gigaFLOPS ^[[ 53 ] ](#cite_note-nvidia.com-53) in double precision calculations, and the AMD FireStream 9270 peaks at 240 gigaFLOPS. ^[[ 54 ] ](#cite_note-ati.amd.com-54)
In November 2011, it was announced that Japan had achieved 10.51 petaFLOPS with its [K computer ](/wiki/K_computer). ^[[ 55 ] ](#cite_note-Petaflops-55) It has 88,128 [SPARC64 VIIIfx ](/wiki/SPARC64_VIIIfx)[processors ](/wiki/Central_processing_unit)in 864 racks, with theoretical performance of 11.28 petaFLOPS. It is named after the Japanese word " [kei ](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E4%BA%AC#Japanese)", which stands for 10 [quadrillion ](/wiki/1,000,000,000,000,000), ^[[ 56 ] ](#cite_note-56) corresponding to the target speed of 10 petaFLOPS.
On November 15, 2011, Intel demonstrated a single x86-based processor, code-named "Knights Corner", sustaining more than a teraFLOPS on a wide range of [DGEMM ](/wiki/DGEMM)operations. Intel emphasized during the demonstration that this was a sustained teraFLOPS (not "raw teraFLOPS" used by others to get higher but less meaningful numbers), and that it was the first general purpose processor to ever cross a teraFLOPS. ^[[ 57 ] ](#cite_note-57) ^[[ 58 ] ](#cite_note-58)
On June 18, 2012, [IBM's Sequoia supercomputer system ](/wiki/IBM_Sequoia), based at the U.S. Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory (LLNL), reached 16 petaFLOPS, setting the world record and claiming first place in the latest TOP500 list. ^[[ 59 ] ](#cite_note-IBM_Computer_Sets_Speed_Record-59)
On November 12, 2012, the TOP500 list certified [Titan ](/wiki/Titan_(supercomputer))as the world's fastest supercomputer per the LINPACK benchmark, at 17.59 petaFLOPS. ^[[ 60 ] ](#cite_note-60) ^[[ 61 ] ](#cite_note-61) It was developed by Cray Inc. at the [Oak Ridge National Laboratory ](/wiki/Oak_Ridge_National_Laboratory)and combines AMD Opteron processors with "Kepler" NVIDIA Tesla graphics processing unit (GPU) technologies. ^[[ 62 ] ](#cite_note-62) ^[[ 63 ] ](#cite_note-63)
On June 10, 2013, China's [Tianhe-2 ](/wiki/Tianhe-2)was ranked the world's fastest with 33.86 petaFLOPS. ^[[ 64 ] ](#cite_note-64)
On June 20, 2016, China's [Sunway TaihuLight ](/wiki/Sunway_TaihuLight)was ranked the world's fastest with 93 petaFLOPS on the LINPACK benchmark (out of 125 peak petaFLOPS). The system was installed at the National Supercomputing Center in Wuxi, and represented more performance than the next five most powerful systems on the TOP500 list did at the time combined. ^[[ 65 ] ](#cite_note-65)
In June 2019, [Summit ](/wiki/Summit_(supercomputer)), an IBM-built supercomputer now running at the Department of Energy's (DOE) Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL), captured the number one spot with a performance of 148.6 petaFLOPS on High Performance Linpack (HPL), the benchmark used to rank the TOP500 list. Summit has 4,356 nodes, each one equipped with two 22-core Power9 CPUs, and six NVIDIA Tesla V100 GPUs. ^[[ 66 ] ](#cite_note-66)
In June 2022, the United States' [Frontier ](/wiki/Frontier_(supercomputer))was the most powerful supercomputer on TOP500, reaching 1102 petaFlops (1.102 exaFlops) on the LINPACK benchmarks. ^[[ 67 ] ](#cite_note-67) ^[ *[circular reference ](/wiki/Wikipedia:Verifiability#Wikipedia_and_sources_that_mirror_or_use_it)*]
In November 2024, the United States’ [El Capitan ](/wiki/El_Capitan_(supercomputer))[exascale ](/wiki/Exascale_computing)[supercomputer ](/wiki/Supercomputer), hosted at the [Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory ](/wiki/Lawrence_Livermore_National_Laboratory)in [Livermore ](/wiki/Livermore,_California), displaced Frontier as the [world's fastest supercomputer ](/wiki/TOP500)in the 64th edition of the [Top500 (Nov 2024) ](/wiki/TOP500).
### Distributed computing records
[ [edit ](/w/index.php?title=Floating_point_operations_per_second&action=edit&section=7)]
[Distributed computing ](/wiki/Distributed_computing)uses the Internet to link personal computers to achieve more FLOPS:
  - As of April 2020 ^[[update] ](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Floating_point_operations_per_second&action=edit) , the [Folding@home ](/wiki/Folding@home)network has over 2.3 exaFLOPS of total computing power. ^[[ 68 ] ](#cite_note-68) ^[[ 69 ] ](#cite_note-69) ^[[ 70 ] ](#cite_note-70) ^[[ 71 ] ](#cite_note-71) It is the most powerful distributed computer network, being the first ever to break 1 exaFLOPS of total computing power. This level of performance is primarily enabled by the cumulative effort of a vast array of powerful [GPU ](/wiki/Graphics_processing_unit)and [CPU ](/wiki/Central_processing_unit)units. ^[[ 72 ] ](#cite_note-72)
  - As of December 2020 ^[[update] ](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Floating_point_operations_per_second&action=edit) , the entire [BOINC ](/wiki/BOINC)network averages about 31 petaFLOPS. ^[[ 73 ] ](#cite_note-73)
  - As of June 2018 ^[[update] ](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Floating_point_operations_per_second&action=edit) , [SETI@home ](/wiki/SETI@home), employing the [BOINC ](/wiki/BOINC)software platform, averages 896 teraFLOPS. ^[[ 74 ] ](#cite_note-74)
  - As of June 2018 ^[[update] ](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Floating_point_operations_per_second&action=edit) , [Einstein@Home ](/wiki/Einstein@Home), a project using the [BOINC ](/wiki/BOINC)network, is crunching at 3 petaFLOPS. ^[[ 75 ] ](#cite_note-75)
  - As of June 2018 ^[[update] ](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Floating_point_operations_per_second&action=edit) , [MilkyWay@home ](/wiki/MilkyWay@home), using the [BOINC ](/wiki/BOINC)infrastructure, computes at 847 teraFLOPS. ^[[ 76 ] ](#cite_note-76)
  - As of June 2020 ^[[update] ](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Floating_point_operations_per_second&action=edit) , [GIMPS ](/wiki/Great_Internet_Mersenne_Prime_Search), searching for [Mersenne primes ](/wiki/Mersenne_prime), is sustaining 1,354 teraFLOPS. ^[[ 77 ] ](#cite_note-77)
